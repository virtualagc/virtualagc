Introduction

Insofar as the Space Shuttle is concerned, the Virtual AGC Project's present goals — or if you'd prefer, my goals — are the following:

  • To provide the complete source code for the software for the most-significant of its onboard computer systems, to the extent allowed by U.S. law.
  • To provide all of the official documentation needed to understand that software and to work with it.  See the References.
  • To provide development tools suitable for working with the software source code, and in particular for compiling/assembling/linking it into executable form.
  • To provide an emulator suitable for running that executable code.
  • For the emulator to be integrated into space-flight simulation systems such as Orbiter+SSU or FlightGear.

In essence, we'd like to do the same kinds of things for the Space Shuttle's onboard computers, and in particular the computers' software, as we have done for Apollo's onboard computer systems and software.

I don't pretend to be putting together an "everything about the Space Shuttle" site.  If you want to know about the Space Shuttle's Main Engines (SSME) or Reaction Control System (RCS), or hear marvelous facts such as the maximum payload size being a 15×60 foot cylinder weighing 65,000 pounds, then this is not the place to look.  (But that's big, isn't it?  I never knew.)

Now, there are various nuances to the statements above, such as whether access to source code must be restricted in some ways, rather than being freely available.  And by "the" source code, do I mean all revisions?  Do I mean for all components of the system?  And by "the" development tools, do I mean the original ones, or do I mean partial work-alikes?  And by emulation, do I mean emulation of the entire stack of code, or just for some restricted portion of it?  And besides which, how do I really know which documents may be relevant to these matters and which may be completely irrelevant?

For example, although I explicitly said above that this isn't the site to come to if you want to learn about engines (SSME), the engines were in fact controlled by a dedicated controller containing two redundant Honeywell HDC-601 digital computers ... so shouldn't those computers and their software be covered here?

Answers to those questions will become clear in the sections below ... or at least, clearer than they are now.  There are a lot of gray areas.  And I don't pretend to know all of the answers yet, so we may need to await future events to have a more-complete picture.  But there aren't necessarily unique, permanently-correct answers anyway.  One thing I can say unequivocally is that integration into space-flight simulation systems is my hope rather than anything that I'll actively pursue personally; integration is the prerogative of the developers of those space-flight simulators, rather than mine, if they feel it's worthwhile for them.  But it's a bit premature to worry about that yet.

The upshot is that my explanation of the Shuttle's computer systems will by necessity be rather limited.  The system is simply too complex, and there are too many resources already available on the web for me to suppose that a presentation by a johnny-come-lately like me would be worthwhile or even interesting about a topic this big.  Perhaps the best place to get a general introduction would be Chapter 4, "Computers in the Space Shuttle Avionics System", of James Tomayko's Computers in Spaceflight: The NASA Experience, but there are numerous other documents in our Shuttle Library to provide more detail.

With that said, here's a brief synopsis.  As with any engineering system of substantial complexity, prepare to descend into acronym hell!

The portion of the Shuttle's full avionics system which primarily concerns us is the Data Processing System (DPS), which includes the General Purpose Computers (GPC), the crew interface (display and keyboards), the mass-memory units, and the data-bus network interconnecting all of them.  Here's a diagram, swiped from the aforementioned Computers in Spaceflight, that gives a very high-level view of the system architecture:



As you can see, there were five separate GPCs.  Each of the GPCs on later flights was an AP-101S computer, designed and manufactured by IBM's Federal Services Division (as were the Apollo LVDC or Gemini OBC, though the GPC was not similar to them in any noticeable way).  Although I may not talk about the AP-101S much, it's worth mentioning that it was a kind of embedded version of the IBM System/360 mainframe, in that it shared roughly the same assembly language, known as Basic Assembly Language (BAL).
Aside:  To be perfectly pedanticaccurate, the GPCs were originally AP-101B computers.  The AP-101S is an upgrade of the AP-101B, replacing 416KB of core memory with 1024KB of semiconductor (CMOS) memory, and possibly other improvements that I've so far not been able to identify for certain, though there are various machine-code instructions that I believe were newly-added in the AP-101S.  The upgrade effort began in 1989, and was first flown in 1991 on STS-37 with software version OI-8F.  More on the topic of flight-software versioning will appear later.
Four of the GPCs nominally redundantly ran identical software, known as the Primary Flight Software (PFS) atop the Flight Control Operating System (FCOS).  PFS and FCOS together are collectively referred to as the Primary Avionics Software Subsystem (PASS). 
Aside: In spite of this technical distinction between the acronyms PFS and PASS, I find in practice (and have been chided by veterans of the Shuttle project) that the term PASS was always used in preference to PFS.  In other words, people speak of PASS vs BFS rather than PFS vs BFS, and stare at you blankly if you mention PFS to them.  Since that is the common usage, I'm going to adopt it throughout the remainder of this article, and will not bull-headedly use the acronym PFS (even though I think it's technically correct) unless the distinction vs PASS is truly significant.
Nominally, the behaviors of these four copies of FCOS were synchronized ... not on a CPU-cycle by CPU-cycle basis, but to the extent that inputs to the GPCs from the spacecraft, as well as commands output from the GPCs to the spacecraft, occurred at the same time.  In particular, the fact that outputs from the GPCs were synchronized allowed detection if one of the GPCs was behaving abnormally.  I say they did this "nominally", because this extreme level of redundancy was warranted only during critical flight phases ... in particular, during ascent and reentry.  During the more-leisurely phases of the mission, if additional computing power was needed, the four principal GPCs did not necessarily need to run identical, redundant software.

The fifth GPC instead ran the Backup Flight Software (BFS), created entirely separately from PASS in a clean-room fashion.  And similarly to PASS, the BFS "application code" ran atop the Backup Operating System (BOS).  This fifth GPC served roughly the same purpose in the Shuttle as the Abort Guidance System (AGS) did in the Apollo LM.  BFS was specialized for abort functionality, i.e., reentry in the absence of a reliable set of GPCs running PASS.  And as I said above, this capability was really (potentially) needed only during ascent or reentry.

The data buses interconnecting the GPCs and peripheral devices, physically and electrically, were MIL-STD-1553 buses.

The crew-interface devices included:
  • 26 line by 51 character displays, capable also of displaying some graphics.  Prior to about the year 2000, the pilots had 3 of these displays and the crew specialist had 1; they were monochrome (green text on black background) cathode-ray tubes (CRTs).  After 2000, the CRTs were replaced by multi-color liquid crystal displays (LCDs), 9 for the pilots and 2 elsewhere.  Collectively, the CRTs and LCDs were referred to as Multifunction Display Units (MDUs). 
  • Keyboards.  The pilots had 2 of these, and the mission specialist station had a 3rd.

The pre-2000 configuration was known collectively as the Multifunction CRT Display System (MCDS), while the post-2000 configuration was known as the Multifunction Electronic Display Subsystem (MEDS).

In the diagrams below, the pre-2000 configuration is shown on the left, while the post-2000 configuration is shown on the right.  Notice that the LCD-based displays (on the right) have 6 buttons along the bottom edges that the CRTs (on the left) lack, as well as being taller relative to their width.  The LCDs continued to display 51×26 textual characters, just as the CRTs had, but the text was scrunched into the upper part of the screen, while a strip along the bottom of the LCD could display additional stuff that the CRTs hadn't been able to, such as menu options selectable by the edge buttons.  These differences were transparent to the PASS / BFS flight software, because the additional stuff displayed along the bottom was not controlled by the PASS / BFS software.  In contrast, keyboards were the same in type and number throughout the duration of the Shuttle program.

Like the AGC, AGS, and LVDC, which were programmed essentially in the assembly language native to their CPU types, the Flight Control Operating System (FCOS) and Backup Operating System (BOS) were written the assembly language of the AP-101S CPU.  But once you get past those infrastructural software components, the bulk of PASS application code was written in a higher-level language called HAL/S, as was the BFS.  The DPS Overview Workbook explains the overall structure of PASS better than I can: 
"PASS software consists of two types of software: system software and application software. System software runs the GPC. It is responsible for tasks such as GPC–to–GPC communication, loading software from MMUs, and timekeeping activities. Application software is software that runs the orbiter. This includes software that calculates orbiter trajectories and maneuvers, monitors various orbiter systems (such as power, communications, and life support), and supports mission–specific payload operations. The application software is divided into broad functional areas called major functions; in turn, each major function consists of Operational Sequences (OPS), which are loaded into the GPCs for each major phase of flight.

"Finally, each OPS has one or more Major Modes (MMs) that address individual events or subphases of the flight."

A Brief Visual Tour: Simulating STS-134

As I explained earlier, we provide an emulator in which the Shuttle's flight software can be run by executing the actual software, on an emulated GPC. Of course, to actually see what's happening, you can't get by with just a GPC, you need emulations of other peripheral devices as well, such displays, keyboards, a control panel, an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), and so on.  A full range of such emulated devices will only be available, I'm sure, when the emulated GPC is integrated into actual spaceflight-simulation systems like Orbiter or FlightGear.  But for demonstration purposes only, we've nevertheless thrown together a set of emulated peripherals that a least allow us to run the GPC emulator through its paces and give you a good idea that it probably works.  What I propose to do in this section is to show you the end result, by running simulations of various aspects of the flight of STS-134 on our makeshift Orbiter simulator, and then presenting videos of the simulations.

STS-134, the next-to-last flight of the entire STS program, was an International Space Station (ISS) mission, having (from my perspective) the following basic phases:

  1. Prelaunch: i.e., Initial Program Load (IPL) and various other setups.
  2. Launch & ascent to orbit.
  3. Climbing to the ISS orbit and matching it.
  4. Approach & docking with the ISS.
  5. Staying docked with the ISS for a long, long time.
  6. Undocking & some associated maneuvers immediately thereafter.
  7. Orbit changes and other preparation for getting back home again.
  8. Deorbit, entry, & landing.

Plus some other fun but counterfactual stuff, like aborting the ascent after launch.  We've simulated all of that stuff ourselves, so we know it works.  But that doesn't mean that it's all equally interesting to the casual bystander. The phases I've emphasized in boldface seem like the interesting ones — and not coincidentally, the ones that take less time —, so we'll just confine ourselves to videos of those. Otherwise, I'd be posting videos of days on end of nothing obvious happening except drifting around in space and occasionally adjusting orbits or attitudes somewhat.  Then too, we don't need a full 5-GPC set to for this tour, so we'll economize a bit: We'll just use a single GPC and as few peripheral devices as we can get away with. For example, because a full Orbiter control panel is quite a large thing, and screen space is quite tight in comparison, we'll show only small, stylized portions of it. If you want to see fully-synchronized sets of GPCs operating in tandem, you'll get a chance to see it elsewhere.

Aside: Just to be clear, our purpose in performing these simulations was to insure that the GPC emulator was as accurate as we were capable of making it, and not to produce cute (albeit rather lengthy) videos. That we were able to produce a video record of it for you was merely a happy (from our perspective) side effect.

Note: It's important to give credit where credit is due, so let me say at the outside that Don Schmidt has made a very outsized contribution to what I'm about to show you. More about his work will be described later, but for now let me just say that without that contribution there would actually be very little to show you.

The prelaunch process takes several hours of more-than-usual tedium, so let's just skip past that.  First up will therefore be launch and ascent to orbit! We'll pick up the story at around T-4:30 (i.e., 4 minutes and 30 seconds before launch).  The whole thing is more-or-less autonomous, although the "crew" will do a few little things along the way.  By "the crew", of course, I mean a script we've written to flip control-panel switches or make keypad entries at appropriate times, since I didn't want to have to do that myself.  By the way, this might look better if you watch it directly at YouTube rather than in the embedded viewer below; watch it in HD (4K or more, if you have it), expand it to fill the whole screen, and be sure to enable CC (subtitles) or else you won't get my wonderfully witty explanations of what's going on.


If you know more about it than I do — which believe me, wouldn't be difficult! — let me know of anything that needs to be corrected. If it's easy enough and important enough, I might actually do it. Probably not, though, because it's a big hassle to produce these things. I already even have a better version of this simulation, made after a number of bug fixes, but the hassle factor is so great that it's just sitting on my local computer rather than at YouTube, and is likely to remain there.

And as long as we're still more-or-less near the launch pad, let's see what it might have looked like if an abort would have occurred. In this next simulation, we start out at about T-00:08 and then launch, but a couple of minutes later force a left-engine fail. This results in the flight being aborted and returning to Kennedy Space Center (KSC), or more accurately going to the Shuttle Landing Facility (SLF), and landing there.  This is called a Return To Launch Site (RTLS) abort.  The landing in this simulation is almost entirely autonomous as well, although in this case that's unrealistic: Beyond the fact that the abort never occurred in STS-134, or in any other flight, flight rules didn't allow the Orbiter to land autonomously after an abort, so the pilots would have taken over. Nevertheless, the Orbiter was technically capable of landing autonomously, and I'm certainly no pilot, so an autonomous landing is what you get.


An RTLS like we just saw wasn't the only kind of abort during ascent. If the failure occurred at a high enough altitude, then the Orbiter just continued into orbit. Not surprisingly, that's called an Abort To Orbit (ATO).  I won't bore you with a simulation of that, but I expect it looks much like any other launch/ascent. Yet a third kind of abort occurred if the Shuttle had gone far enough from the launch site to make RTLS impractical, but hadn't yet gone high enough for ATO. In that case, you could have had a Transoceanic Abort Landing (TAL), which means that the Orbiter continued across the Atlantic to land at a designated emergency site in Europe or Africa. (To complete the full roster, and then ignore it forever, there were also Abort Once Around, AOA, and Contingency Abort, CA.) While several such emergency landing sites were available for TAL, such as Ben Guerir in Morocco or Morón in Spain, I have a sentimental attachment to Zaragoza in Spain, designated ZZA. That's actually a joint civil-military airport, but its runway 30L was available to the Shuttle, so the temptation to make a video for it overwhelmed me.  Here it is, like it or not!


But enough with simulating aborts that never took place in real life!  After the launch & ascent we saw earlier, before the digression into aborts, it took STS-134 close to 44 hours to get into the vicinity of the ISS, in an appropriate orbit, so we'll just skip that. If someone requests a 44-hour video of that ... well, who knows? Perhaps I'd make it for you without subtitles, to reduce my own hassle factor. Why does it take so long? Mostly, I expect, because the ISS is not in a roughly equatorial orbit; rather, its orbit is highly tilted, so that it varies in latitude between something like N60° to S60° on each roughly 90 minute orbit. So you can't just gut it out and blast directly from Cape Canaveral into a matching orbit. It takes some finessing ... and in space, finessing takes a lot of time.  It's like taking an airplane trip (or bus trip) in which you have to change planes (or buses) a lot of times: the trip itself may be short in distance, but all of those transfers really eat up the time.

Instead, we'll proceed to the next phase after that long coast to the ISS, namely approaching and docking with the ISS. Unlike flight phases we saw in the videos above, the Orbiter is not technically capable of docking with the ISS, and requires a pilot. Which is a dilemma, since in spite of all the time you spent watching the videos above, I still have not turned into a pilot in the meantime. The workaround is that we've written a piloting app that we can run alongside the simulation. The app can manipulate the various Orbiter controls just like the pilot can, as well as assessing the data provided to the pilot.  In fact, all it actually does is make small adjustments using the aft Translational Hand Controller (THC).  Make no mistake: The piloting app is not as good as the pilot. But it's good enough to perform the docking, just barely, so it's what you get in the simulation videoed below.  One reason it works is that docking is something that happens very, veeerrrrrrry slllooooowwwwwwwllllllllllyyyyyyyy. We start out about 4300 feet (less than a mile, though more than a kilometer) from the ISS, moving towards it at the pace of a brisk walk (about 6.6 feet/second).  It slows down rapidly from that point onward, and the hard dock is nearly two hours later!

And in spite of what I said earlier, this is one case in which bug-fixes resulted in a significantly improved simulation, so you actually do get an updated video here. Lucky you! Although when I say "significantly improved", I mean that it's significant in my own obsessed little mind. The plain truth is that if you saw the original video, you don't need to watch this one, and indeed might not even notice the differences.


The docking video above, and the undocking video below, mark the first appearance of RPOP.  RPOP is a program for a laptop computer that the Orbiter pilots used during the docking and undocking operations.  RPOP stands for Rendezvous and Proximity Operations Program, and it began to be used in STS-71, in which the Orbiter rendezvoused with Mir. In other words, it's not part of the Orbiter's control panel and doesn't run PASS or be controlled in any way by PASS, though it does have a cabling connection by which it gets data from the Orbiter. As I write this, very few people have seen any of these simulations, but the few who have and who seem compelled to comment about them invariably point to RPOP as being the coolest part of the whole thing. Funny story about RPOP ... I used Claude Code AI agents (and to a much smaller extent Codex and Grok Build agents) to help me create this makeshift Orbiter simulator. One Claude Code agent was tasked with creating the program that displays the views out of the "windows", and the first time I saw RPOP was when I demoed the out-the-window views for myself. My eyes Ron-popped out of my head — get it? RPOP'd? —, because not only hadn't I asked for RPOP, in point of fact I had never even heard of it before then. Upon inquiry, Claude informed me that, yes it was a real thing.  Claude had read about it in the documentation and created RPOP of its own accord, just because it seemed like the right thing to do.  And no we didn't actually have any of the software for it, but Claude had merely followed the documentation, and came out with something that indeed looks pretty close to the real thing. I've asked for a few tweaks since then, some of them very wrong-headed and requiring reversal, but basically RPOP remains as Claude created it, entirely on its own without any suggestion by me.  True story.  On my wish list is actually running that STS-71 rendezvous scenario with Mir, with RPOP, but albeit not likely with the correct PASS version for it. Perhaps someday; we've already prepared the 3D model of Mir for it and it looks pretty good!    But I digress.

Once docked with the ISS on Flight Day 3 (FD 3), the Orbiter stays there quite a while, until FD 15.  We don't bother simulating any of that.  Also, we didn't simulate some interesting preparation for undocking, in that the Orbiter adjusts the attitude of the ISS somewhat. I'll repeat that: The Orbiter adjusts the attitude of the ISS. We have not bothered to model any of that in the simulation because it didn't seem to be worth the effort of implementing the peripherals involved, modeling the dynamics of the ISS, and so on. Perhaps someday we'll do so anyway. At any rate, the undocking itself takes place about an hour after that, and simply assumes that the ISS is already in the appropriate orientation.  Nevertheless, undocking has some other interesting stuff going on that we do simulate.  Immediately after undocking, the Orbiter does a "fly-around" of the ISS, which is nominally a circle with a radius of roughly 650-700 feet. There's a graphic of that to the left.  (The ISS is at 0,0, and isn't shown; note that the horizontal and vertical scales are different, which is why you don't see anything that looks like a circle.) And then after that, STS-134 performed a maneuver known as the STORRM DTO, which is a big, roughly kidney-bean shaped loop that's about 5 miles long by 2 miles wide, continually adjusting the attitude of the Orbiter so that the bay opening is towards the ISS. The first of the graphics to the right depicts the as-flown STORRM DTO trajectory. (Again, note not only that the vertical and horizontal scalings differ, but that they differ from the other graph as well. Both graphics are from an analysis by Stuitt.) The STORRM DTO was part of a continuing series of tests, spread across a number of flights, for testing instruments intended for the prospective Orion spacecraft. But for our purposes today, all that really matters is that the trajectory and attitude of the Orbiter are correct with respect to what STS-134 actually flew. Achieving that turned out to be quite the adventure, and in fact we've never managed fully to do so. There are a number of reasons why that turns out to be tricky, but one big factor is that STS-134's as-flown trajectory as shown in the figure didn't fully match the planned reference trajectory. In fact even contemporary simulations show that a very wide range of outcomes that can be expected in trying to fly it. In the second of the two figures on the right is the planned reference trajectory, with the yellow circles depicting 3σ deviations of the actual positions of the various milestone positions in the trajectory. Quite a wide dispersion! But you can't expect our modern simulator to do any better than NASA was able to do back in 2011, and indeed it will not do so; in other words, our simulated STORRM trajectory will match neither the planned trajectory nor STS-134's as-flown trajectory, and if we run it again we'll get yet some other trajectory for our troubles. Of course, they'll all look the same to the naked eye. Only after milestone SEP3 in the STORRM DTO has been reached does the Orbiter begin to shape orbit for home, and that's where the video below leaves off.  Beware: The video is nearly 5 hours long, because the Orbiter traverses the entire thing (15 miles?) at roughly walking speed!


After that, there's nearly 45 hours of orbit changes in preparation for being in a position to land. We'll skip past that, just like we skipped over the 40+ hours between ascent and approaching the ISS. Instead, we'll just jump ahead to the deorbit/entry/landing phase of the mission.  Like the abort we saw in an earlier video, the Orbiter lands autonomously. This wouldn't normally have happened, because it was deemed that human control would always be safer in the case of events like sudden bursts of wind, but strictly speaking wasn't disallowed by the flight rules. For us, though, it's a necessity, since really-and-truly, I'm still not a pilot.

It is visually different from the earlier abort video, though. For one thing, STS-134 landed on SLF runway KSC 15, whereas we forced the abort simulation earlier to instead land on runway KSC 33. For another, our abort was a day landing, while STS-134's actual landing was at night. The landing is rather exciting in some ways too. The orbiter performed a sequence of very steep rolls, from side to side, in which the wings would be almost vertical, which weren't necessary in the recovery from the abort. This maneuver allowed it to lose altitude very quickly, but if you don't know it's supposed to happen, it looks like a mistake by the simulation; it's, not, though. The Orbiter also comes in quite fast — faster than a human pilot would, I think — so that you have to wonder if it will manage to pull up in time to keep from smacking into the ground at a rather high speed. (Spoiler: No Orbiters were harmed in the making of this video ... just scared into retirement. I did smack it into the ground once at 250 feet/second, and we've never been able to figure out why.)  If you want an incentive to watch the video, that's all I have on tap at the moment.

PASS, BFS, and Other of the Original Shuttle Source Code

I have source code and executable code for some late revisions of the Shuttle's flight software, both primary (PASS) and backup (BFS, partial only), but this fact will turn out not to be as exciting for you personally as it may seem on first hearing it.  So do read on before having an emotional reaction!  The fact that the code survives at all is remarkable, given that a former developer of Shuttle software has told me that:

"When NASA shut down the Space Shuttle project, they erased all of the backup storage media — since there WAS NO REQUIREMENT for saving source code!  Most of the HAL/S compiler and related tools (like ... other support software were not saved), but all of the HAL/S-based flight code was preserved."

In fact, I filed a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request with NASA's FOIA Office to get a copy of the flight software from NASA, but after several months of looking around they asserted that they didn't have a copy of it.  So apparently NASA fully lived up to the lack of a requirement for preserving it, in spite of the assertion of my informant that the flight code had in fact been mysteriously saved (somewhere) after all.  My developer informant also told me that the Shuttle flight-code was the most-expensive software-development project of all time.  Good job all around, U.S. Government agencies, preserving tax-payer investment!

But I digress.

Is this the Original Source Code?

To the extent that we can present the contemporary source code for Shuttle-related software here, or to work with it using the tools provided on this site, some alterations from the original source code files have been needed.  We hope that these changes are not substantive, but a difference is a difference, and you're entitled to know about it if you're interested.

For one thing, Virtual AGC header blocks, consisting of program comments, are added at the top every contemporary file we receive, so that you can understand the provenance of the files as much as possible.  These comments are crafted in a way that lets you distinguish such "modern" comments from the original contents of the files. 

Flight software files, if/when I can eventually show them to you, are expected to be "anonymized" or "depersonalized", so as to remove all personally-identifying information related to the original development teams; thus, whenever the name or initials of a programmer are discovered in the program comments of Shuttle flight software, we have replaced them by a unique but impersonal numerical codes.  This is at the behest of some holders of the original source materials, as a condition for obtaining the software.  Whether this is a temporary or permanent condition, I cannot say.

Most significant, I expect, is the fact that the character encoding of all contemporary Shuttle source code has been completely changed.  This necessity arises directly or indirectly from the fact, unfortunate from our point of view, that the contemporary character-encoding system used was an IBM system called EBCDIC (Extended Binary Coded Decimal Interchange Code), while modern source code (as far as I know) is universally encoded using 7-bit ASCII (American Standard Code for Information Interchange) or an extension of it such as UTF-8.  But EBCDIC and ASCII are essentially 100% incompatible, with only rare, accidental overlaps.  The recoding of the source-code files from EBCDIC to ASCII has been done before we ever received any of the files, and was performed by unknown people, at an unknown time, using an unknown process.  Nor was it always perfectly done, and has required occasional corrections by us.

Moreover, the EBCDIC vs ASCII issue isn't quite as simple as the preceding paragraph suggests, because not all of the EBCDIC characters used originally actually have ASCII equivalents.  There are special considerations regarding how you need to work with HAL/S source code in light of those characters not supported by ASCII.

Here are the general rules:

  1. HAL/S source code should be encoded using 7-bit ASCII characters.
  2. Two characters originally used in HAL/S source code and in the original documentation, namely the logical-not character "¬" and the U.S. cent character "¢", are not present in 7-bit ASCII.  So instead, we use the ASCII characters "~" and "`", respectively, in place of them.   For example, every time you might have seen something like "x ¬= y" in the original HAL/S source code or documentation, we'd expect "x ~= y" instead!
  3. A compiler directive of the form "D VERSION v" was sometimes used in original HAL/S source code or template-library files.  Here, "v" is a numerical version code in the range of 1 through 255, represented as a single EBCDIC character.  To reiterate, a single character position in this compiler-directive string must represent up to a 3-digit version number.  The way they did this originally was simply to pretend that the version code was the numeric byte encoding for a character, and to insert that single-byte numeric code into the string.  For example, if the version was 1, then instead of using the character "1" in the compiler directive, the numerical byte 1 was inserted.  This would have been legal in EBCDIC, even though rather inconvenient since in most cases the character would have been unprintable.  In ASCII or UTF-8, the problem goes beyond that, and such a single-character usage doesn't even represent a valid ASCII or UTF-8 character half of the time.  So we cannot continue to follow this odd practice.  Our change is to instead require this compiler directive to have the form "D VERSION xx", where xx is a 2-character string of hexadecimal digits.

Aside:  With that said, if your operating system supports UTF-8 character coding rather than simple 7-bit ASCII, you can continue to use "¬" and "¢" in HAL/S source code.  The compiler transparently converts them to "~" and "`" during the compilation, and then converts them back to "¬" and "¢" in printouts or in messages it displays.  In particular, this does work fine in Mac OS and Linux, though there may be special considerations trying to do this in Microsoft Windows, discussed later.  In some of the source code we receive, ¬ has instead already been replaced by "^".  Thus any software we provide also silently converts "^" to "~".

A longer explanation is that for some decades now, the most-common character encoding in the U.S. has been 7-bit ASCII, 128 characters in all, sometimes called "plain vanilla" ASCII or just "ASCII".  But since the Space Shuttle's flight software was originally developed on IBM mainframe systems like System/360, rather than using ASCII it used an 8-bit character-encoding scheme called EBCDIC.  It's pretty difficult to find any two EBCDIC tables that agree on all 256 characters, because various IBM systems seemed to have used slightly-different versions of EBCDIC.  But here are ASCII and EBCDIC tables I pulled from Wikipedia that give the basic idea:

HAL/S

HAL/S is a high-level programming language in which the PASS and BFS application software was written.  Whereas infrastructural software (like operating systems and run-time libraries) was written in whatever assembly-languages were native to the particular CPUs running that code.

Here's a brief overview of HAL/S, but if you want to know more, you should look at the dedicated HAL/S page.

HAL/S is a compiled language, and the HAL/S flight-software source code was compiled down to a machine-code executable before it could be run.  Compilers existed for it that could be used on several different types of computers.  Some of the compilers produced code that could be run on an IBM System/360 mainframe; others could produce executable code for the Shuttle's IBM AP-101S onboard computers; others produced executable code for other computers.

I'm sure you can't help but notice that "HAL" was the name of the computer in the movie 2001: A Space Odyssey, which came out in 1968, not too many years before the HAL/S language was invented.  In the movie, H.A.L. stood for "Heuristic Algorithmic Logic", and many people have observed that H.A.L. was just one letter away from I.B.M.  (I.e., "H" is one letter before "I" in the alphabet, "A" is one letter before "B", and "L" is one letter before "M".)  The writer of the movie, Arthur C. Clarke, maintained that that was simply a coincidence.  Where the "HAL" in HAL/S comes from has likewise been explained in several ways, none of them relating to 2001: A Space Odyssey.  The HAL/S language was invented (and the flight software was written) by a company called Intermetrics, many of whose employees were refugees from the same Draper Laboratories (MIT Instrumentation Laboratory) at which the Apollo flight software had been written.  One of those refugees was Ed Copps, one of Intermetrics's founders, who is said to have named the HAL/S language in honor of Hal Laning, perhaps the most-prominent among the designers of the Apollo Guidance Computer's hardware.  Others offer the explanation that HAL/S is an acronym for "High-order Assembly Language / Shuttle".  Still others state that "the acronym 'HAL' was never formally defined".  I have even seen one report (NASA-CR-141758) that refers to it as "Houston Aerospace Language".  Well, who knows?  It's fun to make up your own mind about which constellation of facts matches your own preferences.  Probably not Houston Aerospace Language!  But all I can really say for sure is that there's nothing "heuristic" about HAL/S, even if 2001 may secretly have been somewhere in the back of somebody's mind.

But I digress.  As I was saying, the HAL/S software for some revisions of the Shuttle's PASS and BFS still survives, which is much better than the alternative of it not existing anywhere even if (as I explained earlier) I cannot presently show you any of it.

Working with the PASS/BFS source code requires knowledge of the HAL/S language.  Fortunately, we have a fair amount of documentation of that which you can see:

  1. The recommended starting point is "Programming in HAL/S" by Michael Ryer, which was intended as an introduction to programming in HAL/S and is organized as a tutorial.
  2. A great supplement to Ryer is the course material for "Basic HAL/S Programming" by Craig Schulenberg.  Additional material associated with that course can be found on our Shuttle library page.
  3. Ryer's book points out that it is not a definitive exposition of the language, and recommends proceeding afterward to the "HAL/S Language Specification", which contains a much-more-formal specification of the language syntax, both in the form of graphs of the syntax and in Backus-Naur form (BNF).
  4. Or to the "HAL/S Programmer's Guide".
  5. And then there's the "HAL/S-FC User's Guide" explains how to compile and execute a HAL/S program.  That explanation is, of course, completely irrelevant to our present situation, but you may find that the document answers some questions left unanswered by the preceding documents.  (You many notice as well that our library contains a "HAL/S-360 User's Guide"; that's simply a predecessor of the "HAL/S-FC User's Guide".  The former assumes that the computer on which the compiler ran was an IBM-360, while the latter assumed it was some arbitrary "mainframe" to which the compiler had been ported.)

We actually have quite a few revisions of some of these documents (and others) in our library, spanning the mid-1970's to the mid-2000's, though I've only chosen to link the latest versions of those documents above, even though from time to time the latest revision isn't always the most complete one. 

Here's a brief sample of HAL/S code from "Programming in HAL/S", just to give you its flavor:

  FACTORIAL:
  PROGRAM;
     DECLARE INTEGER,
             RESULT, N_MAX, I;
     READ(5) N_MAX;
     RESULT = 1;
     DO FOR I = 2 TO N_MAX BY 1;
         RESULT = I RESULT;
     END;
     WRITE(6) 'FACTORIAL=', RESULT;
  CLOSE FACTORIAL;

What this program does is to read a number (N_MAX), compute its mathematical factorial, then output the result.  While I won't dissect this short program in detail, I can make a couple of observations.  For one, the language is strongly typed, meaning that every variable has a type that's declared at compile time, and that storage for it is fixed and unalterable at run-time.  Nor is there any dynamic memory allocation (as well as no stack and no recursion), so RAM usage is completely known at compile time.  HAL/S programs never unexpectedly abort because memory has filled up.  The other observation is that the READ(5) and WRITE(6) statements are very familiar to FORTRAN users ... or at least to FORTRAN users of (shall we say?) a certain vintage.  In FORTRAN-speak, the 5 and 6 are "logical unit numbers" (LUN) whose specific interpretation as keyboard and printer (or keyboard and display, or even as files) are assigned externally by the Job Control Language (JCL) used to run the job.  This reflects the fact that the first HAL compilers targeted IBM 360 computers rather than the Shuttle's computers.  In the Shuttle software, these READ and WRITE constructs, I think, wouldn't have been used, and keyboard input or display output would instead have been handled by calls to the run-time library.

HAL/S actually has many novel features not visible in the FACTORIAL example, such as those devoted to real-time response and scheduling of execution.  Again, I won't get into most of those here. 

With that said, perhaps the most-novel feature is a superficial one, namely the ability to express mathematical formulae in a multi-line format that the language's designers felt was more self-documenting than the usual single-line manner of expressing mathematical formulae in programming languages.  One comment made several times in the documentation is that it's worthwhile spending a lot of effort making source code easy to read ... because more time will eventually be spent reading the code than was spent writing it.  I.e., the time lost in creating readable code is more than made up for by the savings in maintaining the code later.  This is sound engineering doctrine, according to the software-design literature of the time ... but very far from today's actual practice and attitudes (2022), in which the initial design schedule is everything, and downstream maintenance is an afterthought performed by somebody management doesn't have to budget time or money for today.  That turns out not to be a problem for HAL/S, though, because while you could input source-code in this supposedly more-readable multi-line format, you weren't required to, and usually did not do so.  Instead, you just input the source code in whatever manner was most convenient and efficient for you.  But regardless, the compiler always "pretty printed" that same source code in multi-line format in its output listings, so you got the benefit of readability without the hassle of personally making it readable.

Were the HAL/S designers on the right side or the wrong side of history in this debate?  (The answer is an exercise for the reader.)

What the multi-line format mainly does is to allow a more-natural representations of superscripts and subscripts.  Here's a HAL/S sample that illustrates the multi-line pseudo-mathematical format:
C Compute corners of a parallelogram.

  CORNERS: PROGRAM;
     DECLARE SCALAR,
                LONG, SHORT, ALPHA;
     DECLARE VECTOR(2),
             AB, BC, CD, DA;
     READ(5) LONG, SHORT, ALPHA;

E    -
M    AB = 0;

E    -
M    BC = VECTOR (LONG, 0);
S               2

E    -
M    DA = VECTOR (SHORT COS(ALPHA), SHORT SIN(ALPHA));
S               2

E    -    -    -
M    CD = BC + DA;

E             -   -   -   -
M    WRITE(6) AB, BC, CD, DA;
  CLOSE CORNERS;
Seeing this example, your immediate reaction is likely: Yes, they were definitely on the right track here!  If so, I'd invite you to consider what the result would be like if any these lines of code exceeded the 72 columns in which they'd normally have to fit on punch cards, and ask yourself if this format were still readable if long statements were wrapped around into one or more additional continuation lines?

What this example program does is to allow input of parameters describing a parallelogram — namely, the lengths of a "short" side and a "long" side (which is assumed to be along the x-axis), and the angle between them in radians — and then to output the (x,y) coordinates of the four corners.  The code also illustrates another of HAL/S's novel features, in that it can do arithmetic not just on scalar variables like integers or floats, but also do vector arithmetic or even matrix arithmetic. For example, vector/matrix addition or subtraction, vector dot products or cross products, matrix multiplication or inversion, etc.  Functions like COS or SIN or VECTOR2 (which forms a 2-vector from two scalar inputs) were available in the run-time library or as compile-time arithmetic when appropriate.

It's important to realize that the lines with the leading characters E, M, and S in the example above are active code rather than merely program comments.  In HAL/S, column 1 has a special purpose.  Normally that column is blank.  True, if a C appears there, it actually is a full-line comment, just like in Fortran.  If a D appears there, then the line is a compiler directive.  But for a multi-line mathematical form, M in column 1 indicates the formula's "main" line, whereas E indicates an "exponent" line and S indicates a "subscript" line.  You can see that several places above.  When this multi-line math format is discussed, it's generally described as a "3-line" format.  But in fact, there was no limit to the number of E or S lines associated with a given M line.  For example, here's some valid code:
E         DEX$J
E       I              I
M   COEF          ALPHA
S                      L
S                       M**2
But you'll notice that if you have a subscript in an E line or an exponent in an S line (as in DEX$J or M**2 above), you just have to live with those little bits remaining in single-line notation.  (In fact, there's not even any real need to put an M in column 1 for a main line, since the M meant exactly the same thing to the original compilers as a blank in column 1.  I'm told — thank heaven! — that nobody ever actually did omit the M's.)

In the multi-line math format, if a variable (like AB) has a '-' above it, that means that AB is really a vector.  Actually, a 2-vector, in the example code above.  That's reflected in the declaration "DECLARE VECTOR(2), AB, ...".

And '-' isn't the only datatype-related character that can appear in the E line above a variable in the M line:
  • '-' for vectors.
  • '*' for matrices.
  • ',' for strings.
  • '.' for booleans.
  • '+' for structures.
Collectively, these were referred to as "overpunches".

My impression is that creating source code in this multi-line format is a pain in the neck, since aligning the columns cards isn't that easy.  But as I said, HAL/S doesn't actually require the use of this E/M/S multi-line format for (input of) mathematical formulas.  A single-line format is perfectly valid as well, and for the CORNERS program would look like this:
  CORNERS: PROGRAM;
     DECLARE SCALAR,
                LONG, SHORT, ALPHA;
     DECLARE VECTOR(2),
             AB, BC, CD, DA;
     READ(5) LONG, SHORT, ALPHA;
     AB = 0;
     BC = VECTOR$2(LONG, 0);
     DA = VECTOR$2(SHORT COS(ALPHA), SHORT SIN(ALPHA));
     CD = BC + DA;
     WRITE(6) AB, BC, CD, DA;
  CLOSE CORNERS;
In the single-line format, The beginning of a subscript is indicated by the '$' character.

One thing you may take away from this is that the overpunches ('-', '*', '+', ...) which appear on the E lines to indicate vector vs scalar variables weren't really needed, since they don't show up in the single-line notation at all; in fact, they're just eye-candy that's nice for readability, and are actually discarded by the compiler.  Whereas the subscript '2' which appears on the S line is in fact quite necessary, since VECTOR() isn't the same thing as VECTOR2(), which isn't the same thing as VECTOR3().

There was also a kind of decoration which the compiler added to invocations of "macros", which were string substitutions (like the #define in C or C++) made on the source code before any actual compilation was performed.  Such invocations were underlined in printouts.

My guess is that almost all source code was written in the single-line format ... and indeed, I've been told by one of the original developers that this is so.  However, realize that yet another novelty of HAL/S is that the original compiler allowed the programmer no control whatsoever over the format of the output compiler listings.  Those were always pretty-printed according to the standards decided upon by the designers of the compiler.  (Well, I think that maybe pretty-printing could be turned off, but that doesn't mean the person writing the code controlled the format.)  

The same is true of any other formatting decisions.  I mentioned above that column 1 of the source code has a special purpose, and thus it matters what characters appear in column 1 vs other columns.  But for all lines which have blanks in column 1, the input source code is completely free-form:  Multiple statements can appear on a single line.  Individual statements can be broken across multiple lines.  Empty lines and whitespaces within lines are ignored, except where at least one space is needed between two adjacent identifiers.  (And except in comments or literal quoted strings.)

The latter point is interesting in connection with the operation of multiplication.  While HAL/S has the usual operators for a lot of mathematical or logical operations, such as "-" for subtraction, "/" for division, "**" for exponentiation, and so on, it has no operator for multiplication.  Multiplication is indicated simply by placing variable names, constant names, or literal numbers adjacent to each other (separated by whitespace).  For example,
DECLARE SCALAR, X, Y, Z;
DECLARE INTEGER, I, J, K;
X = Y Z ;
I = 2 J K ;

By the way, SCALAR is what HAL/S calls its floating-point type; thus SCALAR contrasts with INTEGER or BOOLEAN datatypes, but not with VECTOR or MATRIX.  In fact, all VECTOR and MATRIX objects consist entirely of SCALAR values.  You can't have (say) a VECTOR of INTEGER values.  On the other hand, there is an ARRAY type, of arbitrary dimensionality, which can hold values of any datatype you like, including VECTOR and MATRIX.  And as it turns out, there actually is an operator '*', but it is the vector cross-product operation, not a multiplication of two numbers.  Similarly, the '.' operator is a vector dot product.

Software Versioning

As I've described above, any given shuttle had a number of computers, running a lot of different software components — more than just the GPCs running PASS/BFS we're discussing here.  Each of these software components had their own unique versioning.  I couldn't begin to tell you what those all are; I don't even have a list of all the different computers or their software components, let alone details about their versions.

Multi-Function Display Formatting and Versioning

As was mentioned earlier in the Introduction, the principal method by which the General Purpose Computers (GPC) running the primary flight software (PASS) and backup flight software (BFS) interact with the crew includes keyboards and display screens.  What's unusual about the display screens is that what appears on them is only partially controlled by the PASS or BFS software.  Instead, there was another processor sitting between each of the displays and the GPCs, and it was this extra processor that directly controlled what was displayed and how the display was formatted.  (For that matter, the keyboards also were attached to one of these extra processors rather than to the GPCs, so whatever keystrokes were seen by the PASS / BFS software had already been pre-digested by these extra processors.)

In the case of the older, pre-2000 cockpit configuration (MCDS, 4 CRTs), this extra processor was known as the Display Electronics Unit (DEU), and it consisted of an IBM SP-0 CPU with 8K×16 bits of RAM.  In the case of the newer, post-2000 cockpit configuration (MEDS, 11 LCDs), the extra processor was known as the Integrated Display Processor (IDP), an Intel 368DX microprocessor.  The basic schema is seen in the diagram to the right.  While the diagram is specific to the older (MCDS) configuration, the newer (MEDS) configuration is conceptually quite similar.  In the case of the MEDS configuration, the software for the IDP that was specifically tasked with formatting the display was called the Display Application Software (DAS).  But these kinds of details are of little interest to us in the absence of the DAS or other software that actually ran on the DEU/IDPs.  So the only use of these factoids I'll make in the context of the present discussion is to refer from now on to what I've been calling the "extra processor" instead as the "DEU/IDP".

Aside:  With that said, there's actually quite a lot of information PASS source code about display-screen formats.  Refer to the HAL/S page's discussion of preprocessors.

What is of importance to us, however, is that in addition to inputs from the GPCs via the MIL-STD-1553 databuses, the DEU/IDP's RAM was used to store a set of templates that controlled the formatting of the display screen.  These templates were loaded from mass memory into RAM at power-up.  In other words, the screen templates are independent of the PASS / BFS source code.

For illustrative purposes, there's an example below of the template for screen "GNC SYS SUMM 1" for mission STS-96.  It comes in two varieties, one for the primary flight software, and one for the backup flight software:


PASS GNC SYS SUMM 1 screen, STS-96
BFS GNC SYS SUMM 1 screen, STS-96

As you can probably deduce from these images, some of the areas are supposed to be updated with data from the GPC (or elsewhere in the spacecraft), such as the HH, MM, and SS in the upper-right corner or the X's and S's that are all over the place.  Other markings, like the "SURF", "POS", "MOM", and "DPS" are simply features of the template, and don't change at the whim of the GPC or more specifically, of PASS or BFS.

The screen templates don't quite fall under the Operational Increment (OI) top-level software-versioning scheme we've already discussed.  They do, but they are also controlled by Program Change Notices (PCN).  The examples above are for STS-96 which flew software version OI-27, but that doesn't mean that all missions using OI-27 necessarily had identical screen templates.  In a practical sense, what this means is that to know the screen templates and consequent display-screen formats for any given Shuttle mission, we must have not merely the screen templates for that generic OI, but also the differences to those templates that were made due to specific PCNs ... and of course, actually have the associated documentation so that we can consult it. 

Several documents provide screen templates that can be related to one or more missions or software versions.  The most-available seems to be JSC-48017,  the "Data Processing System Dictionary".  We have several revisions of JSC-48017 in our Shuttle Library, and in principle, if we could collect all of the different revisions, then we'd have all of the screen templates for all of the missions.  The GNC SYS SUMM 1 sample templates above came from one such DPS Dictionary.  There are also reference-card-like summaries that are very helpful, such as this one for OI-34.

On the other hand, the Functional Subsystem Software Requirements (FSSR) documents also contain these templates, and seem a lot more authoritative, as well as providing a lot more information. In fact, the FSSR goes so far as to give screen coordinates for each field, and to explain how every datum received by the DEU/IDP via the databus relates specifically to each X and S on the display screen!  Unfortunately, the FSSRs are also a lot more numerous and a lot harder to find than DPS Dictionaries are, so the dream of obtaining a complete set of them seems more whimsical than obtaining a complete set of DPS Dictionaries.  Nevertheless, on balance, it seems as though the FSSRs should be regarded as the controlling documents for the screen templates.  We just need to collect all of them, or failing that, fall back on DPS Dictionaries when available.  For example, here are the same GNC SYS SUMM 1 templates, but for software version OI-34 (say, mission STS-128), taken from the FSSR.  They're different than the ones shown above for STS-96, though only barely so.  Personally, I see only 4 differences, some sensible, some nonsensical, and some (I suspect) misprints; perhaps you can find more.  Incidentally, STS-96 had the MCDS (pre-2000) cockpit configuration, while STS-128 had the MEDS (post-2000) cockpit configuration, so perhaps that has something to do with the differences.

Computer-to-Peripheral Interface

Keyboard data was supplied to the General Purpose Computers (GPC), and hence to the PASS/BFS software, by means of messages on the MIL-STD-1553 databuses interconnecting the GPCs and DEU/IDPs.  Similarly, data was output by the GPCs for display by passing messages on the databuses as well.  Technical details about this messaging can be found in the Data Processing System Brief.  I won't bother to summarize that information here, since the document's presentation is at least as readable as anything I might write up to supplement it.  While our only available revision of this document so far is for the MCDS, recall that the change from the MCDS to MEDS cockpit configurations was done in a way that was transparent to the existing software.  That implies, I hope, that the messaging format would have been the same in either configuration.

Running the Simulation

In an ideal world, our GPC-simulation software (yaGPC2), or other GPC-simulation software, would be integrated into spaceflight-simulation systems such as Orbiter or FlightGear, and you could fly simulated Shuttle missions executing true PASS using those systems. That's not the world we live in at present ... as far as I know. (Perhaps these systems are just drowning in an abundance of PASS and I'm unaware of it.) Nevertheless, you've already seen in our "brief tour" that there are enough simulated peripherals in the Virtual AGC ecosystem to fly essentially an entire STS-134 mission, at least insofar as guidance, navigation, and control are concerned, for it to act like a somewhat aesthetically-challenged spaceflight simulator. So in this section and its subsections I want to talk just about how to set up a Linux, macOS, or Windows computer to run such simulations.

Important note: The basic elements of the simulation system were not created here at Virtual AGC, but were merely adapted from earlier work by Don Schmidt, which you can see in his own repository. Specifically, Don created the first GPC emulator and MEDS display simulation, as well as contriving the networking interface used to communicate among emulated GPCs and peripherals. Furthermore, his work is the basis for creation of Mass Memory Unit (MMU) "tapes" from which executable code is loaded into the GPC memory, and getting the Initial Program Load (IPL) process to work. Don's work continues as of this writing, and indeed it is possible that you may see better (or at least different) simulation capabilities at Don's website than here. Nevertheless, every element of the simulation here at Virtual AGC has now diverged from Don's work, albeit with great gratitude; all of this may have taken years longer to provide, or may ultimately not have been provided at all, without Don's contribution. The divergence was due to pragmatic issues such as scheduling.

In order to actually run a simulation, besides yaGPC2 and the simulated peripheral devices, several other things may be needed, and are provided (with some provisos necessitated by ITAR and other secular constraints):

  • You need a "tape" for the Mass Memory Unit (MMU), from which flight-software overlays can be loaded into GPC memory as needed.
  • You may need a "snapshot" of the system's state that's near in time to the portion of the simulation you'd like to run. The entire STS-134 mission was about 16 days long, so unless you want to just start at the beginning (Initial Program Load, IPL) and run it for 16 days, you probably only want to run a small portion of it, such as landing. To do that, you can start from a snapshot of the internal state of the mission from shortly before when you want your own simulation to start. Except for the 12-day period when the Orbiter was docked to the ISS, we have snapshots from throughout the mission.
  • You may want scripts to carry out operations during the simulation that you're either not qualified to do, or simply don't want to do. For example, there are scripts to dock with the ISS, undock from it, perform autonomous landings, etc. Depending on how much you're willing to do manually, these scripts may not be needed.

One-Time Setup

  • macOS Golden Gate
  • Windows 11 (native)
  • Windows 11 (WSL)

Basic Simulation

As you may have inferred from the section above, figuring out how to run even a "bare bones" simulation can be quite challenging, because it involves not just running the GPC emulator (yaGPC2), but also a set of desired peripherals, each one of which is generally started in a separate terminal, supplied with tricky command-line options. By a bare-bones simulation, I mean one in which the GPC and a useful set of its peripherals are running and executing flight software, but in which you're largely left to your own devices in figuring out how to do anything of reasonable complexity within the flight software, such as changing orbits, docking with the ISS, or landing it.

To help get around this, there's a program (simulatePASS.py) which runs a bare-bones simulation and deals with most of that complexity. You can use simulatePASS.py --help to see what options are available to you, but bare-bones-or-not, there are still lots of available options.  It can still be quite confusing even in this simplified form, so let's walk through some relatively simple uses.  We'll suppose that you've installed as described earlier, and are in a terminal, in the yaShuttle/discretePanel/ subdirectory in the Virtual AGC source tree as recommended.

First, let's just run simulatePASS.py in its default, most-basic version the command would be:

python3 simulatePASS.py

Dragging the windows is a hassle, but fortunately you only have to do it once, or at most occasionally. Look at the window titled "Manager", at the far right in our sample arrangement. Notice that there's a section in it titled "LAYOUT". If you click the Save button there, it will allow you to save the window arrangement, and let's say you save it as a file called "myDemo.layout".  The next time you run the simulation, you can either use the LAYOUT Restore button to move the windows to where you wanted them, or you can specify the layout by a command-line option:

python3 simulatePASS.py --layout layouts/myDemo.layout

And while we're on the subject of appearance, the default sizes for the various windows are based on my own preferences for my own current computer setup, and may not work well for your particular computer setup. In particular, the windows may be too big or too small for you. Each window is individually resizable by dragging its border, but mostly you'd probably prefer to adjust all of the sizes at once, in a consistent way.  That's done by the --size N command-line option, where 768 is the default "full" size. Personally, I typically use --size 384 to get half-size windows.

python3 simulatePASS.py --size 384

By the way, a stored layout holds not only the window positions, but the sizes as well, and so will override any --size specified on the command line.

Aside: Why 768? It just so happens that 768 pixels was the original size of the MEDS screen during initial development of the simulator. Eventually, it was simply reinterpreted as "full size" rather than as some particular number of pixels.

Another command-line option affecting the appearance in which a purist might be interested is --scale F, where F is a number from 0.0 to 1.0, defaulting to 0.8.  This affects the font-size used in the MEDS (display) windows such as CRT1 in the image above. 1.0 (full size) is probably more-authentic with respect to the original appearance, but in my less-than-humble opinion is ugly and somewhat illegible ... whereas a smaller size is subjectively better as far as I'm concerned.

Okay, enough about the superficial appearance of the thing!  If you look at our sample image above, it's not so impressive in that you see the display (CRT1) showing messages like "MDU IS ANONOMOUS", which is a wonder of minimalist uninformative verbiage.  What it means, though, is simply that the system isn't running yet.  The GPC isn't even turned on yet. In the real Orbiter, you'd now have to go through an intricate Initial Program Load (IPL) process to get the GPC and peripherals turned on and initialized, with the flight software running on the GPC. How do you know how to do that?  Well, we figured out how to do that by walking the checklists given in the PASS User's Guide document. Unfortunately, we don't have one of these for flight software OI-34, but we do have them for OI-20 and OI-32, and the OI-32 version is fortunately close enough to OI-34 reality. Refer to section 2.3 ("System Initialization"), and specifically Table 2-2 ("GPC IPL Sequence"), ignoring the fact that not all of the controls mentioned in the procedure have necessarily been modeled yet in the simulation.

If you're like me, after you've run through this procedure a couple of times, the novelty of it wears off, and you don't want to do it again and again every time you run the simulator.  Fortunately, we've provided a "cheat" for you to work around that. Referring again to the Manager window in the simulation, you'll see a SCRIPT section, with functions Play, Stop, and Record.  If you were to start the Record function before running through the IPL process, a script would be created that held the control-panel switches you had flipped and the keypad keys you had pressed, timestamped, for later playback. Let's say, for example, that you did this and thus created a script called "myDemo.script".  You could later replay that script using the Manager SCRIPT Play function, or else could specify it on the command line, perhaps with the command
python3 simulatePASS.py --layout layouts/myDemo.layout --script scripts/myDemo.script
In point of fact, we've already done this for you, and there's a script you can play back right now. It's not in the default scripts/ directory, but rather in the examples/ directory, but you could play it with the command
python3 simulatePASS.py --script examples/1gpc-1crt-demo-IPL-OPS0.script
Actually, scripts are just text files that you can edit, rather than relying on just "recording" them, and have various additional features you can take advantage of when doing so. (The help-screen printed for python3 simulatePASS.py --help gives a lot of information about how to manually edit the scripts.)  For example, this particular script draws a red circle around each control-panel switch or indicator that it want to call your attention to, which makes it easier to tell what's going on.  While the script is playing, you may notice that at the top of the Manager window there a rundown of which line of the script is currently being processed. You could use the Manager's LAYOUT Stop button to stop the script from being played back as well.  What this script does is simply to run through the entire IPL process, and leaving you at menu screen OPS-000, the top-level menu screen of OPS 0.

If you watched the script playback like a hawk, rather than briefly nodded off to sleep, you may have noticed that once reaching the "GPC MEMORY" menu screen the script performed a number of mysterious operations at the keyboard which the printed GPL IPL Sequence didn't specify.  You may recall that the GPCs mostly interact with each other and with their peripheral devices via networking operations. There are a number of different "databuses", and they are interconnected with physical devices in a flexible way that's determined by something called the Nominal Bus Assignment Table (NBAT).  The simulator's model duplicates the behavior of the actual Orbiter's Data Processing System (DPS). The configuration of the NBAT is basically what's done in the "MEM/BUS CONFIG" column of the GPC MEMORY screen.  (Refer to the image somewhat below.)  It turns out that different OPS often need different NBAT configurations. In particular, OPS in which GPC redundancy/synchronization are needed differs from OPS in which each GPC can basically operate independently.  The extra keypad steps you see when the script runs are the steps for NBAT configuration, although as it happens, those steps are basically just setting the NBAT to its power-up default configuration anyway and so they're not really needed during IPL.

Some scripts take a pretty long time to run, so one thing you can do is to speed up the simulator vs real time, using the --rt-factor X option, where X is a floating-point number. For example,
python3 simulatePASS.py --rt-factor 3.0
attempts to run the simulation at 3× real time. With the particular Linux, macOS, and Windows computers I use for testing purposes, that's actually about the limit of what I can achieve, and your own experience may (and likely will) vary. The limit here is the speed of an individual CPU core, and not the number of cores. In other words, if you have lots of cores, perhaps you could simultaneously run lots of simulations at 3×, but no single simulation could run faster than that. I'm speaking in round numbers, of course, and there's nothing magical about the number 3. Perhaps your own limit will be 2.8 or 1.3, who knows?  But theoretically at least, you can imagine scenarios it which you might want to run the simulation slower than real time, say at 0.5 or 0.25. Why? Well, imagine you were making a video of it and wanted very smooth rendering with no skipped frames. Then perhaps you might want to run the simulation at 0.5× and 15 frames/second rather than 1× and 30 frames/second, to give the simulation more time to do its thing.

Perhaps, though, you don't even have the patience to run one of these scripts, and just want to get to OPS 0 (or wherever!) very fast.  There's a cheat for that as well.  Notice the SNAPSHOT section in the Manager.  The Save function lets you save the entire state of the simulation as a "snapshot", while the Save/Quit function is similar, but also immediately ends the simulation after taking the snapshot.  Let's say, for example, that you take a current snapshot after IPL'ing, and call it "myDemo". It's actually a directory rather than a single file.  The snapshot can later be restored via the Manager SNAPSHOT Restore button, or else via a command-line option:
python3 simulatePASS.py --snapshot-resume snapshots/myDemo

The Wizard

As you saw above, even "bare bones" simulations aren't necessarily simple.  Once you get past the bare-bones stage and start doing stuff that involves the spacecraft's dynamics (rather than just the onboard computers), whole planets, and orbiting space stations, the difficulty steps up dramatically, and consequently the aids I figure it's necessary to give you are somewhat more complex.

To make it a little easier, you can use a program called flightwizard.py in place of simulatePASS.py. The wizard guides you through the process of selecting what options you'd need, depending on what kind of simulation scenario you want to run. Furthermore, to do so, it takes advantage of a library of shapshots of STS-134 simulations that we've already done while testing the software.  The wizard then either prints out the command line you'd use, or else directly runs the simulation for you. (Or both.) Even if you want flexibility that the wizard doesn't provide, you may still benefit by seeing what command the wizard creates for you, and then tweaking that command instead of inventing a new one from scratch.

But first, there's one additional one-time configuration step you might want to perform, using the wizard's help:  As explained earlier, that extra step is configuring the RHCs and THCs to use whatever combination of 3D joysticks, virtual hand controllers, and hotkeys that are most-suitable for your available equipment and tastes.  In the yaShuttle/discretePanel/ directory, just run "flightwizard.py --hand-controllers" to have the wizard guide you through the setup.  I hopefully won't have to explain that configuration process further.  (Knock wood!)

Normally, though, you'd run flightwizard.py to guide you through running a simulation rather than for performing a on-time setup. The wizard remembers your choices, and offers them to you again the next time you run the wizard, though you are allowed to change your choices or to reset to the distribution defaults.

How does this fit in with the access tiers we discussed earlier?  MMU "tapes" and "snapshots" that are needed for running the simulations are encrypted, because they contain executable flight software, and you are given just the password(s) needed for the tiers for which you qualify. Given those choices, you can either locally decrypt/unpack the tapes and snapshot archives, or else you can allow them to remain as encrypted archives. The simulation doesn't really care which. It will check if the decrypted versions are available and use them if so, without prompting for a password. But if not, it will use the encrypted versions, and ask you for a password. If you are at tier 3, the password for the MMU tape and the snapshot archive are the same, so you will be prompted for a single password. If you are at tier 1 or 2, then the MMU tape and snapshot archives will have different passwords, so you will be prompted twice. The passwords are complicated, and a hassle to use, so making decrypted copies is tempting. But just be careful, and if anybody else has access to your computer, perhaps they'd be able to liberate the flight software from you, which would be your responsibility rather than mine. Whereas if you use the encrypted copies all the time, there will never be a locally decrypted copy stored on your computer unless you yourself make your own snapshots of your simulated runs.

Aside: Regarding size, starcore, and stargain, it has proven difficult to come up with some kind of user-interface scheme that works well on all display setups. For example, I use dual 4K 27" monitors, and the defaults are based on that configuration. The size option applies a uniform scaling to all of the windows that comprise the simulator's user-interface, so it's good to use if you can't fit all of those windows onto your display. The simulation, particularly the out-of-window views, can use a lot of graphics RAM, particulary for high-resolution displays like 4K, so for example, you can save a lot of graphics RAM by configuring 4K displays as 1920×1080. This generally looks fine for everything except star fields, which tend to look less crisp than at higher resolutions. The starcore setting can be used to make stars and planets look crisp again at lower resolutions, but with the side effect of making it appear that there are a lot more stars than before, because they appear brighter. The stargain setting can compensate for that by reducing the brightness and, consequently, the number of visible stars.

Aside: Regarding the stop setting, it allows you to select some milestone that when achieved brings the simulation to an end. In general, you have to do this in with flightwizard.py, because the various scenarios are "piloted" by custom-written apps, and so one scenario can't necessarily transition smoothly to the next. Indeed, in some cases there may be access-tier changes that would prevent it even if the piloting apps could handle it. The wizard supplies you with a list of available milestones for the scenario you've chosen, but you can get a full list of these milestones from python3 flightwizard.py --list-options.
Next, if you haven't locally unpacked the encrypted archives supporting your simulation scenario, you are asked to enter a password for any suitable access-tier. For example, for an on-orbit scenario (such as docking), any of the tier-3, tier-2, or tier-1 passwords can be given. Next, if the scenario requires any system snapshots, and the snapshots haven't been locally unpacked, you're asked for the tier-3 password, unless you already gave the tier-3 password before, in which case it's just reused. Sounds complicated, but it's really not.

The simulation then runs. Sometimes it may appear that little is happening, because a raw simulation isn't cropped timewise the way the demo videos you've seen earlier have been. For example, the STS-134 launch occurred at 12:56:28 GMT, but the launch simulation starts at around T-19 minutes, when OPS 1 is entered after the prelaunch setup completes.  Similarly, the launch/ascent scenario doesn't end once you're in "space", which is very quickly, but rather by default when it there's an actual transition from OPS 1 to OPS 2 at around T+53 minutes.  I'd note that the simulator's "Manager" window does have a SPEED setting that you can use to move a little more quickly through these dull periods when nothing much is happening.  But space is big, even if you're just going into LEO at a multiple of real time, and it takes a long time to get around in it.

In general, a scenario simulation tries to show you only those windows (such as portions of the control panel) that it thinks are relevant. In all of the scenarios other than IPL and Prelaunch, it will show you two MEDS, labeled CRT1 and CRT2. As far as the flight software is concerned, assignment of MEDS to different GPCs or for different purposes is very flexible, so I suppose there's not necessarily a "standard" setup for them. Our intention in the setup is that CRT1 will show you the kinds of menu screens you saw so many examples of earlier, while CRT2 will show you the so-called Primary Flight Display (PFD), the main item on which is what's informally known as "the 8-ball", or more-properly the Attitude Direction Indicator (ADI).
Note: If you'd prefer to read something a little more official on this topic than my pre-digested pap, consult section 4.7.1 of the OI-32 PASS User's Guide.
Prior to about the year 2000, the 8-ball was an actual physical instrument on the control panel, but after that the number of display screens vastly expanded, and the 8-ball became virtual: i.e., just a drawing of the 8-ball on one of the MEDS. That's actually set up by using the "edgekeys" along the bottom of the MEDS. You'll notice that one of the edgekeys is labeled FLT INST; pressing that edgekey calls up a different selection of edgekeys, one of which is labeled A/E PFD and the another is labeled ORBIT PFD.  Either can be used, but as you might have guessed, ORBIT PFD is more-suitable for on-orbit operations while A/E PFD is more-suitable for ascent (and launch, abort) or entry (and landing). But it's your choice, and you don't have to abide by the simulator's ideas on the subject.  In fact, you can experiment with MEDS edgekeys all you like while the simulation is running, and you won't affect anything other than what a MEDS is displaying, although I don't guarantee that all of the screens (other than the PFD) that you can select from the edgekeys do what they're supposed to do.  Here's a side-by-side comparison:

Some things that can significantly influence what the 8-ball is showing you are the settings of the various switches in the ADI pane in control panel F8 (for CRT2) or F6 (for CRT1). The ADI ATTITUDE switch has three settings, INRTL (using an inertial reference frame), LVLH (using "level" flight, in which the Earth is always "down"), and REF (using a stored reference frame):

ATTITUDE = INRTL
ATTITUDE = LVLH
ATTITUDE = REF

I think you'd always use LVLH during a landing, and surprisingly you always use it when interacting with the ISS, because the ISS is always in level flight itself.

Then there's the ADI ERROR switch, which has settings HIGH, MED, and LOW.  These adjust the scale of deflection of the 8-ball's error needles  Finally there's the ADI RATE switch, also having settings HIGH, MED, and LOW.  These adjust the scale the 8-ball's own deflection.  To see what I mean, look at any of the screenshots of the ORBIT PFD just above (click to enlarge), for example. There are ruled scales above and to the left of the 8-ball, colored gray, and marked with the limit of 5 at either extreme; ADI RATE changes those markings.  Similarly, there are ruled scales, colored puce, for the error needles, also (purely coincidentally) marked 5; ADI ERROR changes those markings.

I'd note that the simulations are not entirely determinate, and therefore not exactly repeatable. Events such as computer interrupts or receipt  of data over a networking interface are subject to variations, or even inversions of ordering, and while these discrepancies are small at first, they may not be small at all after begin magnified by thousands (or millions) of miles of travel or when they represent attitude errors during burns. This is true not just of our simulations, but was true for the simulations during the Shuttle era as well.

I suppose I should admit that you'll probably be entertained (or not) at some point by flashing error messages that you wouldn't normally have gotten in the real Orbiter, and for which we haven't yet fully worked the reasons. So far at least, just hitting the MSG RESET key on the simulation's keypad gets rid of those messages, with no apparent harm done. If there's a persistent warning tone, seek out the MASTER ALARM button and press it.
